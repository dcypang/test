True-spec 3D physics simulation · flat-bar hybrids

Six hybrids, four roads. One of them is yours.

A six-rider field will race you on real bikes from Cannondale, Specialized, Trek, Giant and Marin. They take wheels, hide in each other's draft, chase you down when you get away and empty the tank in the last few hundred metres. Pick your bike, then use the arrow keys (or the on-screen pad) to pedal, brake, back up and steer anywhere on the map — and sit in their slipstream to save a third of your drag. Speed, the leaderboard and the graph all update live. The engine models aerodynamic drag, tire rolling resistance, gearing, sprung-mass suspension dynamics, bump-impedance losses, grip-limited cornering and W′ sprint stamina — all from each bike's published spec sheet, on procedurally generated terrain.

How the physics works

  • Longitudinal: m·dv/dt = Fpedal − ½ρ·CdA·v² − Crr·m·g·cosθ − m·g·sinθ − Fbump − Fbrake. Pedal force is capped by rear-wheel traction (μ·load) and by gearing (spin-out at ~110 rpm in top gear).
  • Rolling resistance: per-tire Crr (G-One Allround 35c ≈ 0.0045, Pathfinder 40c ≈ 0.0055, Terreno Dry ≈ 0.0070 on smooth asphalt) scaled by surface: knobs are a penalty on tarmac but an advantage on loose ground.
  • Suspension: each bike is a sprung mass (heave + pitch) on two spring-damper corners — tire casing stiffness in series with the Suntour 50 mm coil fork (Quick CX) — plus a separate 20 mm Future Shock DOF at the bars (Sirrus X). Bump energy dissipated in dampers and the rider's body is charged back as impedance drag; the vertical acceleration at saddle and grips gives the ride-comfort RMS.
  • Control envelope: on rough or loose ground each bike gets a max controllable speed from tire grip, tread and suspension travel; the rider AI brakes ahead of tree gates, rock gardens and washboard using a μ-limited braking envelope, exactly like a real rider scrubbing speed before trouble.
  • Live telemetry: every rider's speed, the position-ordered leaderboard and the graph all refresh on the render frame, and traces are sampled on simulation time rather than distance so a stopped rider still plots. The graph's Live view is a rolling 40-second window that scrolls as you ride; Whole course shows speed against distance for the full route.
  • 3D scene: the road mesh is the exact heightfield the physics integrates over — wheels, fork travel and Future Shock motion you see are the simulation state. Asphalt, gravel, forest floor, bark and foliage textures are synthesized at load time from fractal value noise (the page ships no image assets).
  • Rider: 75 kg, steady power (slider), coasts past spin-out, brakes at up to 0.65·μ·g.
  • Your bike: steered with a kinematic bicycle model (ψ̇ = v·tanδ/wheelbase) on the same suspension and drag physics. Steering lock tightens with speed; cornering draws on the same friction circle as drive and braking, so grip spent leaning is grip you no longer have for power — ask for more than μ·g and the front washes out. Sprinting spends a 20 kJ W′ anaerobic reserve that refills only below threshold, and leaving the pavement puts you on the verge's own rolling resistance and grip.
  • Racing you: in Race mode each rider has a brain rather than a metronome. Rae (Quick 2) is a pure roadie who lights it up on tarmac and suffers when it turns rough; Sam (Sirrus X 4.0) is a tactician who hides in wheels and kicks inside the last 170 m; Kit (Quick CX 2) is fearless once the ground gets ugly; Yuki (Trek FX 3) attacks from distance and dares the pack to come across; Bo (Giant Escape 3) is a diesel who never leaves a wheel and is deadly in a drag race off that 48T ring; Ines (Marin DSX 1) comes alive the moment the tarmac ends. They watch your position, raise power to chase when you get up the road, move across to take a wheel when one is available, and spend their own 20 kJ reserve to do it. Drafting follows the wind-tunnel numbers for road cyclists — about 38% less aerodynamic drag at a metre off the wheel, ~25% at three metres, gone by ten, and worth nothing if you are not lined up behind them. It works for you too: sit in and watch your drag drop. Switch to Time trial to turn all of this off and get the clean, identical-rider bike comparison back.
  • Staying upright: you are held to the same control limit the AI respects — the speed at which your tires, tread and suspension travel can still hold that ground. Ride 35% beyond it on loose or broken surfaces, land a jump with more than 3.2 m/s of vertical speed, or hold a slide for half a second, and you go down and remount. It is why holding full throttle down the singletrack is slower than riding within the bike: over 1.2 km the rigid Quick 2 crashes 22 times flat-out versus 6 ridden with restraint, while the 50 mm-travel Quick CX 2 gets away with 9 — the suspension is buying you control, exactly as it does in the real world.
